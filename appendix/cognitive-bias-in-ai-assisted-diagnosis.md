# Cognitive bias in AI-assisted diagnosis

AI assistance can reduce disagreement while introducing new errors when a reader follows an incorrect suggestion. Agreement with other readers, agreement with a reference, confidence, and diagnostic accuracy therefore need separate evaluation.

## A pathology example

Frei and colleagues compared tumor-cell-fraction estimates with and without a computer-aided tool in 10 colorectal-cancer regions of interest. Participants included medical students, residents, and practicing pathologists. Variability decreased with assistance, but for one image some students and trainees moved toward the tool's incorrect output even though their original estimate was closer to the reference. [Frei et al., 2025; PMID 40610733](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00428-025-04163-w).

The experiment used a fixed sequence of unassisted then assisted assessments and a small selected ROI set. It supports concern about incorrect-suggestion uptake in that setting; it does not estimate its frequency across routine whole-slide diagnostic practice. [Study methods](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00428-025-04163-w).

## Questions for a reader study

- What errors are corrected by assistance, and what new errors appear?
- How often does a reader abandon a correct initial judgment after an incorrect suggestion?
- How are reader experience, task difficulty, and uncertainty recorded?
- Are case order, learning, recall, and reader/case dependence addressed in the design and analysis?
- Does any reduction in variability also improve accuracy against an appropriate independent reference?

These are study-design prompts, not a validated bias scale. See [[ai-study-appraisal]] for applicable reporting and appraisal resources.

## Review scope

The cited paper's abstract and methods were checked on 28 September 2026. This is one bounded empirical example, not a systematic review of cognitive biases. Numerical extraction, formal appraisal, and comparison with other human–AI experiments remain to be completed.
