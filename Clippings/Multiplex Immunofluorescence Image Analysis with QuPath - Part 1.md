## Summary

An introductory, open-access workshop that takes a complete beginner from installing QuPath to
quantitatively inspecting multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) images, then on to tissue and cell
segmentation, phenotyping, and cell-composition analysis. Part 1 — the portion covered by the
Zenodo deposit and the eight published videos — deliberately spends its whole length on *digital
images as such*: pixels, bit depth, histograms, channels and look-up tables, dimensions and spatial
calibration, file formats, annotation, and image quality. The mIF-specific work is Part 2.

Two design choices stand out: terminology is anchored to three external bioimage-analysis
references rather than to QuPath's own vocabulary, and image/channel quality assessment is the
first step of the mIF workflow, ahead of segmentation.

## Stated learning objectives (Part 1)

From the Zenodo record, participants completing Part 1 will:

- Install and navigate the QuPath software environment.
- Understand how fluorescence images are generated by microscopes and detectors.
- Learn the fundamentals of digital images: pixels, image dimensions, data types, image histograms.
- Understand visualisation concepts — brightness, contrast, channels, colours, look-up tables (LUTs).
- Learn about image metadata, spatial calibration, multidimensional image data, and projections.
- Compare common bioimaging file formats, including compression, OME-TIFF, and OME-Zarr.
- Create annotations and extract measurements within QuPath.
- Apply best practices for quantitative image inspection and scientific image presentation.

## Module 1 — Workshop Introduction

*Digest below is written from the video's auto-generated captions plus the published module
description; it is not a transcript.*

### Where the workshop starts and stops

The stated arc is: set up and navigate QuPath and its surrounding open-source ecosystem →
acquire the vocabulary of digital image analysis (channels, colours, dimensions) and see how each
term maps onto a workflow step → view, interact with, and annotate images → quantitatively inspect
them for quality → apply all of it to mIF images through tissue and cell segmentation,
classification and phenotyping, and presentation of results.

What is explicitly *out of scope* is as instructive as what is in. The workshop ends at **cell
composition** — a table of phenotyped cells per tissue compartment. Everything downstream of that
(spatial statistics, cell–cell networks, niches, published cellular-neighbourhood methods) is
named, positioned as question-dependent, and deferred to advanced modules that are described as in
preparation. The workshop's deliverable is framed as the *objects* that make such analyses possible
rather than the analyses themselves.

### Terminology is borrowed, not invented

Module names and definitions are taken from three external resources, with an explicit warning that
other sources define the same terms slightly differently:

1. The **Network of European BioImage Analysts (NEUBIAS)** and its bioimage-analysis terminology.
2. **Pete Bankhead's** book on analysing fluorescence microscopy images with ImageJ — Bankhead being
   the developer of QuPath.
3. The **Carpentries** lesson *Image Processing with Python*.

The caution given is practical: within the workshop these resources' definitions are the ones in
force, and the same terms carry slightly different properties elsewhere in the literature.

### What "mIF image analysis" is taken to mean

Analysing multiple fluorescent signals within a single digital image to extract meaningful data.
Four aspects are called out as the things that make it a distinct problem:

- **Spectral unmixing** — separating the wavelengths of overlapping fluorophores so that each marker
  can be quantified as the biology it is supposed to report.
- **Intensity extraction** — reading fluorescence intensity as a proxy for underlying biology.
- **Automation** — using software to visualise, identify, quantify, and measure at a scale humans
  cannot.
- **Spatial relationships** — how the different signals are arranged relative to one another.

### The generic image analysis workflow (four steps)

1. **Pre-processing** — enhance marker signal and suppress background and noise; the
   signal-to-noise ratio is the quality currency.
2. **Segmentation** — identify the image features that correspond to the biology of interest.
3. **Object formation and post-processing** — merge features into objects that can be manipulated
   and measured, with quality control applied to the object set.
4. **Measurement and comparison** — quantify the final objects and compare across tissue types,
   conditions, or experimental arms.

### The mIF workflow used in this workshop (six steps)

Demonstrated on a single tissue-microarray core:

1. **Quality checks** — is the image good enough to analyse at all, and did every marker work? This
   step refines the image down to the channels that can be pursued.
2. **Tissue segmentation** — define the analysable area and, where relevant, separate tissue
   compartments. Automatable.
3. **Artifact removal** — mostly a manual step, with partial automation; further refines the tissue
   area.
4. **Cell segmentation** — identify individual cells within the retained tissue or compartments.
5. **Cell classification / phenotyping**.
6. **Cell composition analysis** — quantify and compare composition between compartments or areas.

Compared with the generic four-step workflow above, this one front-loads quality assessment: the
channel set is settled before any segmentation is attempted.

## Why this is worth keeping

- It is the rare mIF course that treats **image literacy as the prerequisite**, not the appendix:
  eight modules and over two hours before a single cell is segmented. Bit depth, LUTs, spatial
  calibration, and OME-TIFF/OME-Zarr are handled as analysis-determining facts rather than
  file-handling trivia.
- **Quality assessment gates the pipeline.** Channels that failed are dropped before segmentation,
  which is the honest alternative to carrying a bad marker through to a phenotype table.
- **Licensing is genuinely open** — CC BY 4.0 slides on Zenodo with a DOI, MIT-licensed exercise
  files, free software — so the material can be reused for local teaching with attribution.
- It complements the LJI *From Samples to Knowledge* QuPath course: that one is an advanced
  two-day workshop assuming comfort with QuPath, this one is the on-ramp to it.
