## Summary

A review in *Archives of Pathology & Laboratory Medicine* about a circularity at the heart of validating digital tissue image analysis (tIA). To show that an image-analysis tool is analytically valid you need a reference method, and in anatomic pathology the obvious reference is the experienced pathologist's score. But tIA is brought in precisely because that score is subjective, less reproducible, and shaped by known biases of human visual assessment — and because the software can produce measurements no human observer can produce at all. Using the pathologist as ground truth therefore grades the tool against the very errors it was adopted to remove. The authors call this the **gold standard paradox**.

Rather than proposing a replacement reference, the review surveys the literature on traditional, subjective scoring paradigms and the visual and cognitive traps that affect them, and argues that anyone validating a tIA tool against pathologist scores must keep those traps in view when interpreting the tool's reported accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity.

## The argument in four steps

1. **Why quantification.** Targeted therapies act on complex cellular mechanisms, so the biomarkers that guide them are complex too, and the subtle phenotypes that inform treatment decisions increasingly need quantitative methods such as tIA to be read at all.
2. **Why a gold standard.** A tIA system has to be analytically validated against some reference method.
3. **Why the pathologist.** Conventional histopathologic scoring by an experienced pathologist is the accepted gold standard in anatomic pathology, which makes it the natural candidate for ground truth.
4. **Why that is a paradox.** tIA is used to assist scoring *because* it is more objective and reproducible than visual evaluation alone, overcomes known biases in how humans see tissue, and generates endpoints no human observer can. A reference built from human scores carries exactly those biases into the validation.

**Objective of the review:** to discuss the common visual and cognitive traps in traditional pathology-based scoring paradigms that may distort how the accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of tIA-assisted scoring are characterized.

**Conclusion:** awareness of the paradox is necessary whenever traditional pathologist scores are used to analytically validate a tIA tool.

## Reading notes

*My own notes on applying the paper's point — not content taken from the review.*

- **Disagreement is not automatically algorithm error.** When a tIA score and a pathologist's score diverge, the discordant cases are exactly where the paradox bites. A headline concordance figure cannot say which side is wrong; adjudicating the discordant cases says more than the agreement statistic does.
- **Accuracy metrics inherit the reference's error.** Sensitivity and specificity measured against an imperfect reference are biased estimates — typically understated when the tool's errors are independent of the reference's, and overstated when both share the same errors. Inter- and intraobserver variability of the manual score sets a practical ceiling on the agreement any tool can show with a single pathologist.
- **Endpoints without a human equivalent cannot be validated against a human.** Continuous stain-intensity measures, whole-slide per-cell counts, and spatial metrics have no manual gold standard to begin with, so they need a different validation strategy — for example, control material with known expression, or an orthogonal assay.
- **Where this shows up in practice:** IHC H-scores and percentage-positivity estimates, Ki-67 proliferation indices, and any QuPath-style workflow whose output is benchmarked against a pathologist's estimate.
